How to Deep-Clean an Air Fryer

This assignment asked us to write a set of step-by-step instructions for a process our audience might not know how to do safely. I wrote a guide on deep-cleaning an air fryer since it's something a lot of people skip until something goes wrong, and most of the guides online either skip important steps or don't explain why each one matters.

What you'll need: non-abrasive sponge, soft-bristle toothbrush, dish soap, baking soda, microfiber cloth, wooden skewer.

Avoid steel wool or rough scrubbers as they will scratch and damage the non-stick coating.

DANGER: Never submerge the main unit in water. The internal motor and fan are not water resistant.

WARNING: Make sure the air fryer has been unplugged and unused for at least 30 minutes before starting.

Phase 1: Preparation

  • Unplug the appliance and move the cord away from the sink area.

  • Remove the basket and tray and set them in the sink.

  • Wipe the exterior with a damp microfiber cloth and a small drop of dish soap. Stick to the outer shell only.

  • Clean the screen with a dry or barely damp cloth. Don't spray anything directly onto the interface.

  • Clear the vent slats at the back using the wooden skewer and toothbrush to dislodge any trapped dust or grease.

Phase 2: Cleaning the Basket

  • Soak the basket in hot water with a small amount of grease-cutting dish soap for 10 minutes.

  • Make a baking soda paste by mixing baking soda and water at a 3:1 ratio.

  • Apply the paste to any burnt or dried-on grease spots and let it sit for 10 minutes.

  • Scrub gently with a non-abrasive sponge in circular motions, then rinse with warm water. Repeat if needed.

Phase 3: The Deep Clean

  • Flip the main unit upside down so the interior ceiling faces up.

  • Locate the heating coil, the metal ring sitting just behind the fan.

  • Scrub the coil with a toothbrush dipped in soapy water using gentle strokes.

  • Wipe the interior ceiling with a slightly damp sponge. Grease buildup here is a fire risk so be thorough.

  • Dry completely with a clean paper towel before reassembling. No moisture should remain on the heating element.

Phase 4: Reassembly and Testing

  • Air dry all parts for at least 30 minutes. Trapped moisture can cause electrical shorts.

  • Reinsert the basket and plug the unit back in.

  • Run a test cycle at 400°F for 1 to 2 minutes with no food inside. This burns off any leftover soap residue.

Troubleshooting

Problem Cause Fix
White smoke during use Excess grease on the coil Repeat Phase 3
Lingering food smell Trapped crumbs in the vents Re-soak basket with water, lemon juice, and dish soap

Federated Learning for Privacy-Preserving Urban Heat Island Prediction

Undergraduate Research Proposal · Faculty Mentor: Dr. Liqiang Wang · UCF Department of Computer Science

Background & Motivation

Urban heat islands are areas in cities that stay significantly warmer than surrounding rural zones, and they're a growing public health concern. Cities collect temperature, traffic, and land-use data at scale, but sharing that data across municipal boundaries raises real privacy and sovereignty questions. Most existing predictive models either rely on centralized datasets that aren't realistically available, or sacrifice accuracy by working with coarse aggregates.

Federated learning offers a way out of this tradeoff. Rather than pooling raw data in one place, each city trains a local model and shares only the model updates, keeping the underlying data local while still contributing to a shared global model. This project proposes to test whether a federated approach can match centralized model performance on urban heat island prediction tasks using publicly available satellite and sensor data from multiple U.S. cities.

Research Questions

Primary: Can a federated learning framework achieve comparable predictive accuracy to a centralized model for urban heat island mapping, without requiring direct data sharing between participating nodes?

Secondary: How does model performance degrade as the number of federated nodes increases, and what aggregation strategies best preserve accuracy under realistic non-IID data distributions?

Proposed Methodology

The study will use publicly available MODIS/Landsat satellite imagery and EPA air quality sensor data across 10 to 15 U.S. cities. A baseline CNN regression model will first be trained centrally as a performance benchmark. A federated version of the same architecture will then be trained using the FedAvg aggregation algorithm, with each city's dataset treated as a separate client node. Both models will be evaluated on a held-out test set using RMSE and spatial prediction maps.

All experiments will be implemented in Python using PyTorch and the Flower federated learning framework. Simulated federation will allow controlled testing of different numbers of clients, data heterogeneity levels, and communication round configurations.

Expected Outcomes

I expect the federated model to perform within 5 to 10% of the centralized baseline while maintaining full data locality. If successful, this would provide practical evidence that federated methods are viable for urban sensing tasks, with real implications for city planners, public health researchers, and anyone working on climate adaptation infrastructure. The work would also produce a reusable open-source pipeline for federated geospatial modeling that other researchers could build on.
